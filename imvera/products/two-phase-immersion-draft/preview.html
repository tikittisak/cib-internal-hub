How two-phase immersion works

A closed-loop, phase-change cooling mechanism — different from S-Series' single-phase pumped loop.

STEP 01

Submerge

IT hardware fully submerged in a low-boiling-point dielectric fluid (typ. 40–60°C boiling point at 1 atm), inside a sealed tank.

STATE Liquid
STEP 02

Boil

Heat from CPU/GPU/components boils the fluid directly at the hot surface. Phase-change absorbs far more heat per unit fluid mass than heating a liquid without boiling it.

STATE Liquid→Vapor
STEP 03

Condense

Vapor rises to a condenser coil connected to the facility water loop; gives up heat and condenses back to liquid.

STATE Vapor→Liquid
STEP 04

Return

Condensed fluid drips back into the tank by gravity — no pump needed in the primary loop, unlike S-Series' pumped single-phase design.

STATE Liquid

The honest trade-offs (not just the upside)

A fair draft shows the downsides too, not only the pitch.

Sealed tank required

Vapor must stay contained, so the tank can't be opened casually like a single-phase bath — hot-swapping components is less convenient than S-Series' more open tank design.

Real trade-off

Dielectric fluid supply

The two-phase segment's fluid supply has been in flux since 3M announced (2022) it would exit PFAS-based fluid manufacturing (including Novec) by end of 2025. Which fluid/supplier IMVERA would actually use is unresolved.
